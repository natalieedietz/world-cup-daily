First, What You Missed · Saturday 11 July

Two extra-time thrillers completed the final four, and both were gloriously dramatic.

Golazo of the Tournament
Quarterfinal · Kansas City · after extra time
ARG Argentina 3–1 Switzerland SUI
Julián Álvarez's 112th-minute wondergoal finally broke ten-man Switzerland.

Argentina, who appear physically incapable of winning a knockout game the easy way, did it the hard way again. Alexis Mac Allister headed them in front early from a delicious Messi corner, Dan Ndoye leveled it for Switzerland just after the hour, and then Breel Embolo got himself sent off for a dive (a genuinely magnificent piece of self-sabotage), leaving the Swiss to defend for their lives with ten men. They very nearly did, too, until Julián Álvarez uncorked an outrageous curling thunderbolt in the 112th minute that will be replayed for years, before Lautaro Martínez tapped in a late rebound to make it 3-1. Messi's record scoring streak ended at nine games, but he pulled the strings all night. The champions stagger on, as is their custom.

Bellingham, Again Quarterfinal · Miami · a.e.t.
ENG England2–1Norway NOR

England did England things, but survived. Andreas Schjelderup's cross-shot embarrassingly looped over a static Jordan Pickford to give Norway a shock lead, and Haaland and company threatened to run riot. But Jude Bellingham, who is quietly having a monstrous tournament, equalized just before the break and then bagged the extra-time winner to complete his second brace of the knockouts. Haaland, the tournament's viral darling, goes home without a semifinal. England march on to a grudge match for the ages, with manager Tuchel calling the win "lucky" and "sloppy," which is the most English reaction imaginable.

The Storylines

The Grudge Match to End Them All

Argentina versus England is not just a football match, it is a forty-year-old scar with a kickoff time. This is the fixture of Maradona's "Hand of God" in 1986, of David Beckham's red card in 1998, of decades of mutual, cheerful loathing. England have not beaten Argentina at a men's World Cup since 2002. Now they meet in a semifinal, Messi against Bellingham, history against hunger, with a place in the final at stake. If you only watch one game this week, and honestly you should watch both, make it this one. The tension will be visible from space.

Club Connections

Peak star power. France roll out Kylian Mbappé (Real Madrid), Ousmane Dembélé (Barcelona) and Michael Olise (Bayern); Spain counter with Lamine Yamal (Barcelona), Pedri (Barcelona) and Rodri (Man City). Wednesday, Argentina bring Lionel Messi (Inter Miami) and Julián Álvarez (Atlético Madrid), while England answer with Jude Bellingham (Real Madrid), Harry Kane (Bayern Munich) and Bukayo Saka (Arsenal).

Where to Point Your Eyeballs

Both semifinals kick off at noon Pacific, which is a gift to the West Coast and a minor tragedy for anyone with a Tuesday or Wednesday job. Tuesday's France v Spain is the tactical connoisseur's dream, two teams who barely concede and pass opponents dizzy. Wednesday's Argentina v England is the one your group chat will not stop buzzing about. Two lunchtime blockbusters, one long-awaited final on the horizon. Clear the calendar, or at least find a very understanding manager.

The Golden Boot Endgame

Down to the wire, and it is a superstar showdown. Messi and Mbappé are tied at the top on eight goals apiece, with Mbappé now one strike from Messi's all-time World Cup record of 21 career goals. Both are still alive, both are ferociously motivated, and if they each keep winning, they could yet meet in the final with the Golden Boot on the line too. Bellingham and Kane lurk just behind for England. Every goal from here rewrites the record books. Keep score.

France v Spain

This is the one the entire bracket has been quietly building toward since the draw was made in December, the two pre-tournament favorites, first and second on every oddsboard, colliding a round earlier than everyone wanted. France have been the team of the tournament, imperious and faintly terrifying, outscoring opponents 16-2 and not conceding a single goal across three straight knockout wins. Kylian Mbappé and Ousmane Dembélé have five goals each, the first French pair to manage that since Brazil's Ronaldo and Rivaldo in 2002, and Michael Olise has been quietly pulling strings. On current form, Les Bleus look like a team strolling toward a coronation.

Spain, though, are the one side genuinely equipped to stop them, and they have receipts: they beat this France team 2-1 at Euro 2024 and again in a bonkers 5-4 Nations League thriller in 2025, so Yamal and company will feel no fear whatsoever. La Roja play the most distinctive football left in the tournament, a hypnotic, possession-drunk style that uses the ball itself as a defensive weapon, starving opponents of the game. They have conceded exactly one goal all tournament, to Belgium, and their whole plan is to make France chase shadows for ninety minutes and quietly lose their minds. The catch: injuries to Nico Williams and Yeremy Pino have left them short of width, and Yamal has been a touch quiet in the knockouts.

This is, by some distance, the hardest game of the tournament to call, a genuine coin flip between the game's most fearsome attack and its most miserly, controlling machine. France are narrow favorites on current form and firepower, but Spain have the tactical identity and the recent head-to-head record to believe. Expect a tense, high-quality chess match where the first goal is enormous and the margins are razor-thin, and do not be shocked if it goes to extra time or beyond. Whoever wins becomes the overwhelming favorite to lift the trophy in New Jersey. This is a final masquerading as a semifinal. Savor every second.

Upset Watch

Calling Spain an "upset" is generous, they are the second favorites for the whole tournament, and they have beaten this France side twice in two years. If they can hypnotize Les Bleus with possession, protect that miserly defense, and get Yamal firing again, they can absolutely reach the final. The only worry is their injury-thinned attack against a French machine in ruthless form.

Where to even begin. Argentina against England is one of the sport's great blood feuds, a fixture drenched in four decades of history: Maradona's "Hand of God" and his miraculous solo goal in 1986, David Beckham's red card and subsequent national villain era in 1998, Beckham's redemption penalty in 2002. England have not beaten Argentina at a men's World Cup since that 2002 night, and now the two nations meet in a semifinal, with a place in the final and roughly a century of grudges on the line. You could bottle the tension and sell it as an energy drink.

The football is fascinating, too. Argentina, the defending champions, have been the tournament's great survivors, needing extra time in three of their four knockout ties and generally making life as difficult for themselves as humanly possible, but they keep winning, because they have Lionel Messi. He may have seen his record scoring streak end against Switzerland, but he produced arguably the individual performance of the round, pulling every string. England, meanwhile, are quietly the form horse nobody quite trusts: Thomas Tuchel's side have matched their best-ever World Cup, five wins, on the back of Jude Bellingham's heroics and Harry Kane's goals, even if their own manager keeps calling their performances "sloppy." Bellingham has back-to-back knockout braces, a feat last managed by, of all people, Maradona in 1986.

The bookmakers actually make England slight favorites, a rare thing against Argentina, largely on the back of their squad depth, midfield power, and the champions' habit of living dangerously. But Argentina have Messi, tournament know-how, and the priceless ability to win ugly when it matters, and they will relish being underestimated one more time. Expect a tense, cagey, emotionally enormous occasion where a single moment of Messi magic or Bellingham brilliance settles everything, and do not rule out extra time or penalties, because these two do not do simple. Whatever happens, history will be made. Do not miss a single second of this one.

Upset Watch

The twist is that Argentina, the defending champions, are the slight underdogs here, which tells you how much England are fancied. But underestimating Messi in a knockout game has ended badly for two decades of opponents. If he conjures one moment, or the champions drag it to the penalties they have mastered, England's long history of semifinal heartbreak could come calling once again.
