On Your Doorstep
Quarterfinal · Inglewood (SoFi)
ESP Spain 2–1 Belgium BEL
Merino strikes late again. Spain reach the semifinals from a quarterfinal in our backyard.

The World Cup came to Inglewood and did not disappoint. Spain and Belgium served up a proper quarterfinal at SoFi, and once again it was the unlikely boot of midfielder Mikel Merino, not teenage sorcerer Lamine Yamal, who settled it, popping up with a late winner to send La Roja through 2-1. Belgium, to their credit, made Spain sweat and did not go quietly, but Spain's relentless control eventually told. If you made it to the stadium, congratulations, you watched a World Cup quarterfinal fifteen minutes from your house. If you did not, pretend you did. Spain march on to a mouthwatering semifinal, and the ball, as ever, did all the running.

Ominous Quarterfinal · Boston
FRA France2–0Morocco MAR

France continue to look like a team playing a slightly different, easier sport. Kylian Mbappé missed a first-half penalty (Bounou guessed right, briefly giving Morocco hope and the neutrals a giggle), then made amends with a gorgeous curled finish on the hour, his eighth of the tournament and 20th of his career, one behind Messi's all-time record. Six minutes later he turned provider for Ousmane Dembélé, and that was that: a tidy, clinical, faintly terrifying 2-0. Morocco, the last African side standing, bow out with heads high. France look like they mean business.

England, improbably, arrive as the good story, which is exactly when England fans reach for the antacids. After surviving a ten-man, altitude-soaked classic against Mexico at the Azteca, exorcising forty years of Hand of God trauma in the process, Thomas Tuchel's side look tougher and more resilient than the usual England vintage. They have Jude Bellingham pulling the strings, Harry Kane hunting goals, and genuine squad depth, and they are into a third straight World Cup quarterfinal. There is one wrinkle, though: defender Jarell Quansah is suspended after his red card against Mexico, forcing a reshuffle at the back at the worst possible time, because the man they must now contain is a monster.

That monster is Erling Haaland, who has been utterly unplayable, already knocking out five-time champions Brazil with a brace and boasting a preposterous 62 goals in 54 games for his country. Norway are in their first-ever World Cup quarterfinal and playing with house money and enormous belief, orchestrated by Arsenal captain Martin Ødegaard. Their plan is elegantly simple: keep it tight, feed the giant, and let physics do the rest. The problem for England is that a reshuffled defense against the form striker on the planet is the stuff of goalkeeping coaches' nightmares. Norway are leaky at the back themselves, mind, so this could get gloriously silly.

England have more all-around quality and depth, and on balance they should have enough to edge a tight, tense affair, particularly if they can starve Haaland of service and let Bellingham and Kane do their thing at the other end. But Haaland is precisely the kind of player who makes "on balance" meaningless: one half-chance and the entire calculus flips. Expect England to try to control possession and smother the supply lines, expect Norway to sit in and spring the big man on the counter, and expect a nervy, absorbing contest that could very easily go the distance. And if penalties arrive, well, England supporters have a rich and traumatic history with those. Relaxing stuff.

Argentina are somehow still here, and "somehow" is doing heroic work in that sentence. The defending champions have survived their last two knockout games by the skin of their teeth, needing extra time to see off Cabo Verde and then an absolutely barmy three-goal blitz in the final stretch to overturn a 2-0 deficit against Egypt. It has not always been pretty, and the defending has been genuinely chaotic, but they have Lionel Messi, who at 39 is co-leading the Golden Boot race with eight goals and has scored in a record eight consecutive World Cup matches. As long as he is on the pitch, Argentina are never, ever out of it, a fact opposing coaches find deeply annoying.

Switzerland, meanwhile, are the ultimate party-pooper, and they have reached their first quarterfinal since 1954 by doing the least glamorous thing in sport: refusing to lose. They bored Colombia into a goalless stupor over 120 minutes before winning the shootout, and that is essentially their whole personality, organized, disciplined, defensively impregnable, and thrilled to drag anyone into the lottery of penalties. Granit Xhaka runs the show and Manuel Akanji anchors the back line, and they will look at Argentina's leaky defense and fancy their chances of frustrating the champions into another sweaty, nervous night. If any team can bore Messi into submission, it is these joyless geniuses.

Argentina are favored on star power and tournament pedigree, and Messi remains the ultimate trump card, capable of settling any game in a single flash of the divine. But Switzerland are precisely the sort of stubborn, well-drilled, shootout-ready side that could make this deeply uncomfortable, especially given how shaky Argentina have looked at the back. Expect the champions to eventually find a way, because they always somehow do, but do not rule out extra time, penalties, and a fresh gray hair for every Argentine supporter alive. Two great escapes in, a third would test even Messi's supernatural luck. Then again, betting against the man has bankrupted skeptics for twenty years.

Upset Watch

Genuinely plausible. Argentina have been living dangerously, their defending is a shambles, and Switzerland specialize in punishing exactly that kind of disorder by keeping it tight and marching to penalties, where they just beat Colombia. If the Swiss can smother Messi and drag this to a shootout, they have a real path. The champions look beatable, if only you can survive Messi first.
