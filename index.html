Dream Over
Round of 16 · Houston
MAR Morocco 3–0 Canada CAN
Morocco become the first African side to reach back-to-back World Cup quarterfinals.

The co-host's Independence Day party got comprehensively crashed. Canada actually started brightly, buzzing around and threatening for a lively first half, but committed the cardinal sin of not scoring when you are on top, and Morocco made them pay in the most emphatic terms. Azzedine Ounahi scored twice after the break, Soufiane Rahimi added a stoppage-time dagger for good measure, and a promising 0-0 curdled into a chastening 3-0. Morocco, ever ruthless, march into the quarterfinals as the first African nation to reach the last eight at two World Cups in a row. Canada go home with their heads high and a nation newly in love with them. Noble in defeat, which is very on-brand.

Theatrical Round of 16 · Philadelphia

France advanced, though the game will not be troubling any highlight reels titled "The Beautiful Game." Paraguay did what Paraguay do, defending grimly and treating the pitch as a soft place to lie down, and the match descended into a chippy, whistle-heavy festival of tumbling. Kylian Mbappé settled it from the penalty spot in the 70th minute, reclaiming his Golden Boot lead in the process, and France ground out a deeply French 1-0. They face Morocco next, presumably with ice packs.

The Storylines

Man Versus Machine Versus Brazil

Erling Haaland has scored five goals at his first World Cup and found the net in every single game he has started, which is the kind of statistic that makes opposing center-backs phone in sick. He is enormous, absurdly fast, and finishes chances with the cold efficiency of a man ticking items off a grocery list. Standing in his way: Brazil, five-time world champions, who would very much prefer he stop. The catch is that Haaland has faced Brazil's Gabriel eleven times in the Premier League and scored six. The defender knows him well. That has not historically helped.

Club Connections

An embarrassment of Premier League riches. Brazil roll out Vinícius Júnior (Real Madrid) and Matheus Cunha (Manchester United); Norway counter with Erling Haaland (Manchester City) and captain Martin Ødegaard (Arsenal). Mexico lean on Santiago Giménez (AC Milan) and Edson Álvarez (West Ham), while England, as ever, bring an entire league's worth: Jude Bellingham (Real Madrid), Harry Kane (Bayern Munich) and Bukayo Saka (Arsenal).

Where to Point Your Eyeballs

Both games are in gloriously civilized West Coast hours, so you can watch all four hours of drama and still be in bed at a reasonable time. The 1pm Brazil v Norway is the connoisseur's pick, two thrilling attacks and a very real chance of a goal-fest, especially with New Jersey baking in mid-90s heat that no defender will enjoy. The 5pm Mexico v England at a heaving Azteca is pure theatre, history, hostility and a red-hot host nation. Fire up the group chat.

Ghost Watch · The Azteca Remembers

England are walking back into the Estadio Azteca, the precise stadium where, forty years ago, Diego Maradona knocked them out of a World Cup with the "Hand of God" and then, four minutes later, the greatest goal ever scored, just to twist the knife. England have presumably not forgotten. The Azteca certainly has not. Now they must win there, at altitude, against a Mexico side that has not conceded a goal all tournament, roared on by a crowd that considers English misfortune a national pastime. No pressure, lads.

On paper, this is the tie of the round, and for once the paper is not lying. Brazil are Brazil, five-time world champions, blessed with a front line of ludicrous quality in Vinícius Júnior and Matheus Cunha, and freshly reminded of their own mortality after needing a 95th-minute winner to squeak past Japan in the last round. Under Carlo Ancelotti they remain gloriously talented and occasionally, thrillingly, a little bit shaky at the back, which is precisely the sort of flaw that a certain Norwegian is engineered to exploit. They are narrow favorites, and they know a shootout suits nobody's blood pressure.

Because Norway are not here to admire the samba. They are here because Erling Haaland has spent this tournament treating goalkeepers like traffic cones, banging in five goals and scoring in every game he has started, ably supplied by his Arsenal captain Martin Ødegaard, who passes like a man threading silk. This is Norway's first World Cup knockout appearance since 1998, and they have arrived with a swagger, having won all eight qualifiers with a goal difference that reads like a typo. The plan is not complicated: get the ball to the big man and see what happens. It usually works.

The one wrinkle Norway have already been shown is that an elite attack can overrun them, France put four past them in the group stage, and Brazil carry exactly that kind of firepower. Add mid-90s New Jersey heat that will have both sets of defenders melting by the hour mark, and every indicator points to goals, drama, and a real possibility of extra time if Haaland does Haaland things. Expect Brazil to edge a gloriously open game, but do not be even slightly surprised if the big Norwegian drags his nation into the quarterfinals by sheer force of will. This is the one to clear your afternoon for.

Upset Watch

Extremely live. Norway are only marginal underdogs, and they possess the single most in-form striker in the tournament plus a midfield conductor in Ødegaard. Brazil's defense has wobbled, the heat favors chaos, and if Haaland gets two clean looks he tends to take them. A Norwegian quarterfinal would be a shock, but nobody would call it a fluke.

Some fixtures come pre-loaded with drama, and this one arrives absolutely stuffed. England return to the Estadio Azteca, the very ground where in 1986 Diego Maradona punched a ball into the net, called it the Hand of God, and then scored the greatest goal in World Cup history four minutes later, sending England home and into four decades of therapy. Now they are back, at 7,200 feet of lung-burning altitude, in front of a Mexican crowd that has waited forty years to enjoy English suffering all over again. If atmosphere were a substance, the Azteca would be arrested for possession.

The football is a genuine puzzle too. England, marshaled by Jude Bellingham and finished by Harry Kane, are the bookmakers' slight favorites and boast a squad deep enough to field two competent teams, though they retain their traditional gift for making straightforward games look like open-heart surgery. Mexico, meanwhile, are the story nobody saw coming: the first team since Italy in 1990 to win its opening four World Cup matches without conceding a single goal. That is not a defense, that is a vault. Backed by a delirious home crowd and the thin mountain air that leaves visitors gasping, El Tri fancy this enormously.

Expect tension, expect noise, and expect England to sweat for every yard, literally and figuratively. On paper the Three Lions have more individual quality and should have enough to edge through, but the Azteca is the great equalizer, and a Mexico side that simply refuses to concede is exactly the kind of stubborn, spirited opponent that turns a favorite's evening into a nightmare. This has penalties written all over it, which, given the two nations involved, is a sentence to strike fear into roughly 130 million hearts. Unmissable television.

Upset Watch

Very real, and the bookmakers barely separate these two. Mexico have not conceded all tournament, they have the altitude, and they have a crowd that will make England's ears ring. If El Tri score first and the Azteca erupts, England's famous tournament nerves could resurface at the worst possible venue. This is a coin-flip dressed as a favorite's game.
